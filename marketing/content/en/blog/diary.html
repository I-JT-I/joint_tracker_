Why keeping a consumption diary changes things

Almost everyone thinks they know their own habits well, until they start writing them down. The gap between how much you think you use and how much you really use is one of the most common — and most useful — effects of keeping a diary.

Assumed habits vs real habits

Memory tends to remember the "exceptional" sessions better (a particular night out, an unusual weekend) and to underrate the daily routine, which goes more unnoticed precisely because it's repetitive. The result is a distorted picture: you underestimate how often you usually use and overestimate the weight of one-off episodes.

What changes when the data is objective

Having real numbers — sessions per week, grams per month, recurring times — lets you make informed decisions: working out whether it's time for a tolerance break, whether your monthly spending is higher than you thought, or whether a certain context (stress, boredom, socializing) drives most of your use.

It's not only for people who want to cut down

A consumption diary isn't only for people who want to use less: it's also useful just to know yourself better, to understand what does and doesn't work for your downtime, or simply to have a record of when and where you use, like a map of your regular spots.

Why do it in an app instead of in your head

The main problem with "keeping it in your head" is that the details slip away fast: after a few weeks it's impossible to remember exactly how many sessions there were, how often, or how much you spent. An app like JointTracker logs each session in a few seconds and works out stats, charts and streaks automatically, so the data stays accurate with no effort of memory.
